Saqqara (the Serapeum)

Memphite burial ground of the Apis bulls, Egypt, used under Persian kings, 6th-5th century BCE

In the game: Level 2: The Spear-bearer

Saqqara, on the desert edge west of Memphis, was the necropolis of the Memphite kings and nobles. In the area later called the Serapeum, the Apis bulls, living manifestations of the god Ptah, were buried in underground galleries. Stelae from these burials date particular bulls by regnal year and include the Persian kings in the Egyptian reckoning.

The Apis galleries

In the record

Excavations at Saqqara, begun by Auguste Mariette in the early 1850s, uncovered long rock-cut galleries where Apis bulls were laid in great stone sarcophagi. Chapels and stelae stood above and around them. The galleries were extended over centuries, and burials of the Persian period lie among them.

A. Mariette, Le Sérapéum de Memphis

Stelae dating the bulls

In the record

Dedicatory stelae record the birth, installation and death of particular bulls, with dates by regnal year. Burials of bulls fall in the reigns of Cambyses and of Darius. These are Egyptian documents written under Persian kings, and they give the kings the usual titles of Egyptian pharaohs.

M. Malinine, G. Posener and J. Vercoutter, Catalogue des stèles du Sérapéum de Memphis, 1968

Cambyses and the Apis

Disputed

Herodotus says Cambyses stabbed a newly found Apis in the thigh in a fit of madness, and that the bull died. The Saqqara evidence does not support this: a bull buried in Cambyses' reign received the proper rites, and no stela shows an insult to the cult. Most historians treat Herodotus's story as hostile Egyptian tradition about Cambyses, though the question is still argued, in particular over which bull the dates fit.

Herodotus 3.27-29

Darius and the cult

In the record

A stela from the reign of Darius records an Apis burial, which shows the cult continuing under Persian rule. This fits other evidence that Darius supported Egyptian temples and law, such as his work on the House of Life at Sais. The Persian kings, in these texts, kept the bull's rites as pharaohs had done.

Catalogue des stèles du Sérapéum de Memphis, 1968

Name and later history

Ancient account

The name Serapeum belongs to the Greek-speaking world of later centuries, when the cult of Osiris-Apis was linked with the god Serapis. Strabo describes a Serapeum at Memphis, partly buried in sand. His account is of his own day, not of the Persian period.

Strabo 17.1.32
