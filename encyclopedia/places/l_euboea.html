Euboea

Long Aegean island off Attica and Boeotia, site of Eretria and Artemisium, 490 and 480 BCE

Euboea is the long island that lies along the east coast of mainland Greece, separated from it by a narrow strait. The Persians destroyed its city Eretria in 490 BCE, and off its northern shore the Greek fleet fought Xerxes' ships at Artemisium in 480 BCE. The island therefore figures in both Persian invasions.

An island beside the mainland

Ancient account

Herodotus describes Euboea as an island lying close to the mainland, and his narrative of 480 BCE has fleets moving along its coast. Eretria and Chalcis are its best-known cities in his account. The narrow channel between island and mainland is the one later called the Euripus.

Herodotus 8.1-21

Eretria in 490 BCE

Ancient account

Herodotus says the force of Datis and Artaphernes sailed to Euboea after Naxos and the islands, and that Eretria resisted for six days. He reports that on the seventh two prominent Eretrians betrayed the city, that the temples were burned and that the people were enslaved. He gives the cause as Eretria's help to Sardis in 498 BCE, when it sent five ships to the Ionians.

Herodotus 6.98-101

Eretrians resettled

Ancient account

Herodotus says Darius settled the captured Eretrians at Ardericca in Cissia, not far from Susa. He adds that their descendants were still living there in his own day and still spoke Greek. The detail rests on Herodotus alone.

Herodotus 6.119

Artemisium in 480 BCE

Ancient account

Herodotus places the Greek fleet at Artemisium, on the north coast of Euboea, while Leonidas held Thermopylae. He tells of a Persian squadron sent round the south of the island that was wrecked by a storm, and of fighting on several days that ended without a clear result. The Greeks then withdrew when the pass fell.

Herodotus 7.175-195

Numbers and motives

Disputed

The sizes Herodotus gives for the Persian and Greek fleets are hard to check, and historians generally think his totals for the Persian fleet are inflated. The plan to trap the Greeks by sending ships around Euboea is known only from his account. Modern writers also differ on how to judge Artemisium, since the Greek retreat followed the loss of Thermopylae rather than a defeat at sea.

P. Briant, From Cyrus to Alexander, 2002
