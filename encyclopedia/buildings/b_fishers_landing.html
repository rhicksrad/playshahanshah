Fishers' Landing

Fishing on the rivers, marshes and seas of the Achaemenid world, 6th to 4th century BCE

Fish fed many of the empire's people, from the Babylonian marshes to the Nile and the Aegean coast. Greek writers describe whole communities who lived on it, and the Persian treasury drew money from Egypt's fisheries. Few sources say how ordinary fishers worked, so much of their daily life is known from older tomb paintings and from the salted fish shipped across the sea.

In the game: The landing stands with its back at the water's edge and sends small boats out to net fish, which carts take to a granary. Households eat fish or meat for up to half their ration. This is a simplification: many fishers worked from the bank with nets and traps, and no source describes fishing on the rivers of Persis itself, so a landing there is our inference.

The fish of Lake Moeris

Ancient account

Herodotus says the water of Lake Moeris in the Fayum ran in for six months and out for six. While it ran out, he says, the catch brought a talent of silver a day to the royal treasury, and twenty minae a day while it ran in. He adds that Egypt's tribute to Darius of 700 talents came on top of the money from the lake's fish. His figures may be inflated, but they show a fishery treated as a source of state income.

Herodotus 2.149, 3.91

Peoples who lived on fish

Ancient account

Herodotus says three Babylonian tribes ate nothing but fish, dried in the sun, pounded and sifted through linen. He says Egyptians ate fish dried or salted, though priests refused it. Around 325 BCE Alexander's admiral Nearchus reported Fish-eaters on the Gedrosian coast of the Arabian Sea, who used nets of palm bark and ground dried fish into meal. These are outsiders' reports and tend to make such peoples sound strange.

Herodotus 1.200, 2.37, 2.77; Arrian, Indica 29

Skiffs and nets on the Nile

In the record

Egyptian tomb reliefs show fishers in light papyrus skiffs hauling drag nets between two boats, or working with hand nets, baskets and lines. The Old Kingdom tomb of Ti at Saqqara has a well known example. These scenes are far older than the Persian conquest and were made for the tombs of the rich, but papyrus skiffs and nets remained in use on the river, and Herodotus describes the marsh people's nets in his own day.

Mastaba of Ti, Saqqara; D. J. Brewer and R. F. Friedman, Fish and Fishing in Ancient Egypt, 1989; Herodotus 2.95

Salted fish across the sea

In the record

Salting let fish travel. A building at Corinth held jars from the western Mediterranean in the 5th century BCE, and fish bones in them point to a trade in preserved fish. Herodotus has a guard at Sestos in 479 BCE frying salted fish when, he says, it leapt in the pan. The evidence is thin for how much of this trade reached the towns of the Persian empire, and most people probably ate what was caught close to home.

M. L. Zimmerman Munn, Corinthian Trade with the Punic West, 2003; Herodotus 9.120
