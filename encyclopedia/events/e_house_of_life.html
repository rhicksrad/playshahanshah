Darius's restoration of the House of Life at Sais

Royal order to revive the House of Life, probably at Sais in Egypt, about 519-518 BCE

In the game: Level 5: The Charter

Early in his reign Darius ordered the Egyptian official Udjahorresnet to leave the Persian court and restore the House of Life, probably the one at Sais, a temple institution of sacred writing and medicine that had fallen into decay. The order is known almost entirely from Udjahorresnet's own inscribed statue, which also shows how Persian kings worked with Egyptian temple elites to govern Egypt.

In the game: The game shows this as a decree of 518 BCE sending Udjahorresnet home. The statue gives no year, so 518 BCE is a modern estimate, and the House of Life was a temple scriptorium of sacred learning rather than simply a school.

The statue and its text

In the record

Udjahorresnet had served as a naval commander under the last Saite kings, Amasis and Psamtik III. After Cambyses conquered Egypt in 525 BCE he became chief physician and a court companion, and he composed the King's Egyptian throne name. His career is told in hieroglyphs carved on a statue of him holding a small shrine. It is now in the Vatican Museums, probably brought to Italy in Roman times.

Naophorous statue of Udjahorresnet, Vatican Museums

Darius's order

In the record

The inscription says that Darius, while he was in Elam, ordered Udjahorresnet to go to Egypt and restore the House of Life after it had decayed. Foreigners escorted him from land to land. He says he staffed it with students from respected families, set them under learned men and supplied all their needs. He adds that the King acted because he knew the value of this craft for keeping the sick alive.

Naophorous statue of Udjahorresnet; A. Kuhrt, The Persian Empire, 2007

What a House of Life was

In the record

Egyptian texts from many periods name the House of Life as a body attached to temples where scribes copied, studied and kept sacred books on ritual, medicine and magic. It was less a school in the modern sense than a workshop of written knowledge. The statue does not name the town, but scholars usually place this one at Sais, beside the temple of Neith, whose cult Udjahorresnet had already persuaded Cambyses to protect.

Naophorous statue of Udjahorresnet; A. H. Gardiner, "The House of Life", Journal of Egyptian Archaeology 24, 1938

Egyptian doctors at the Persian court

Ancient account

Herodotus tells that Amasis sent an Egyptian eye doctor to Cyrus at the King's request. He also says Darius kept Egyptian physicians at court, reckoned the best in medicine, though they failed to treat his twisted ankle until the Greek Democedes cured it. Whatever the truth of the details, his stories fit the statue's claim that Darius valued Egyptian medicine.

Herodotus 3.1; Herodotus 3.129-132

Collaborator, patriot or both

Disputed

Historians disagree on how to read Udjahorresnet. Some see a collaborator excusing his service to the conquerors; others a local notable protecting his temple and town as best he could. His picture of Cambyses honouring Neith clashes with Herodotus's sacrilegious Cambyses. Scholars also debate the date of the order, usually placed in 519 or 518 BCE, and whether the great turmoil he mentions means the conquest of 525 BCE or the revolts of 522 BCE.

A. B. Lloyd, "The Inscription of Udjahorresnet: A Collaborator's Testament", JEA 68, 1982; P. Briant, From Cyrus to Alexander, 2002
