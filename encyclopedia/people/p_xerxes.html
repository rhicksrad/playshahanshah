Xerxes

King of the Persian empire, son of Darius and Atossa, reigned 486-465 BCE

In the game: Level 4: The Terrace, Level 9: The Canal, Level 10: The Hot Gates, Level 11: The Finale, Mycale

Xerxes (Old Persian Xshayarsha), son of Darius and Atossa, ruled the empire from 486 BCE until he was killed in a palace plot in 465 BCE. He put down revolts in Egypt and Babylonia, completed and extended the palaces at Persepolis, and in 480 BCE led the invasion of Greece that took Athens but failed at Salamis. Greek writers made him a model of royal pride, while his own inscriptions present a pious king continuing his father's work.

In the game: The game's chronicle note sums up his reign from Herodotus books 7-9: Egypt's revolt, the Athos canal, the Hellespont bridges, the taking of Athens in 480 BCE and his return home after Salamis. It leaves out the Babylonian revolts.

Chosen heir of Darius

In the record

In an inscription from Persepolis (XPf), Xerxes states that Darius had other sons but, by the will of Auramazda, made him the greatest after himself. The same text traces his line through Darius and Hystaspes to Arsames. Darius died late in 486 BCE, and Babylonian documents soon begin to be dated by Xerxes' reign.

XPf

Revolts in Egypt and Babylonia

Disputed

Herodotus (7.7) says Xerxes crushed the Egyptian revolt begun under Darius and made his brother Achaemenes governor. Babylonian tablets name two rebel kings, Bel-shimanni and Shamash-eriba, probably in 484 BCE, after which several family archives end. Herodotus (1.183) says Xerxes took a gold statue from Bel's sanctuary, and Arrian later claimed he destroyed the temples. Historians doubt a general destruction and still argue over his reprisals.

Herodotus 7.7, 1.183; Arrian, Anabasis 7.17; C. Waerzeggers, The Babylonian Revolts against Xerxes and the End of Archives, 2003/2004

Builder at Persepolis

In the record

At Persepolis Xerxes completed the Apadana begun by his father, built the Gate of All Lands named in its inscription (XPa), and added his own palace. In the XPh inscription he says that in one land the daiva had been worshipped. He says that by the favour of Auramazda he destroyed their sanctuary, forbade their worship and worshipped Auramazda there. The land is not named, and scholars do not agree which event, if any, it describes.

XPa; XPh; E. Schmidt, Persepolis I, 1953

The invasion of Greece

Ancient account

Herodotus tells how Xerxes had a canal cut behind Athos and bridges of boats laid over the Hellespont, then marched through Thrace in 480 BCE. After Thermopylae his army took Athens and burned the Acropolis. When his fleet was beaten at Salamis he returned to Asia, leaving Mardonius in Greece. Herodotus (7.186) puts the host above five million, a figure historians regard as greatly exaggerated.

Herodotus 7.22-24, 7.33-36, 7.186, 8.53, 8.97-120

Lashing the Hellespont

Later legend

Herodotus (7.35) says that when a storm broke the first bridges, Xerxes had the strait given three hundred lashes and the overseers beheaded. Aeschylus' Persians, staged at Athens in 472 BCE, shows a king humbled by the gods for his pride. Such scenes served Greek moral lessons about arrogance and are not confirmed by any Persian source.

Herodotus 7.35; Aeschylus, Persians
