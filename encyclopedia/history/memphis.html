Encyclopedia › The History

Cyrus took Media, Lydia and Babylon. Egypt, the oldest kingdom of all, was left to his son. In 525 Cambyses crossed the desert, broke Pharaoh's army at Pelusium and took Memphis after a siege. He ruled as Pharaoh, and the Greeks remembered him as a madman. The Egyptian records tell a different story.

The narrator

Herodotus says Menes, the first king of Egypt, built Memphis; in 525 its White Walls fell to Cambyses.

Before Egypt: the empire of Cyrus

In the record

Within twenty years Cyrus took Media (550), Lydia (about 547) and Babylon (539). Only Egypt was left of the old great powers, ruled by Amasis, a friend of the Greeks. Cyrus died on campaign in 530, and his son Cambyses inherited the plan.

Nabonidus Chronicle; Cyrus Cylinder; Herodotus 1.153, 3.1

A deserter shows the way

Ancient account

Herodotus gives several reasons for the war, among them an Egyptian princess sent in place of Amasis's daughter, and doubts them himself. The firmer story is Phanes of Halicarnassus, a Greek captain of Amasis's mercenaries, who fled to Cambyses and told him how to cross the desert.

Herodotus 3.1-4

Water in the desert

Ancient account

Three days of waterless desert lay between Gaza and Egypt. Cambyses swore a pact with the king of the Arabs, who loaded camels with skins of water and met the army in the desert. Herodotus also reports, and does not believe, a pipeline of ox-hides from a distant river.

Herodotus 3.4-9

Pelusium, 525 BCE

Ancient account

Amasis died before the Persians came. His son Psamtik III, six months a king, met them at the Pelusiac mouth of the Nile with Egyptians and Greek and Carian mercenaries. The mercenaries killed Phanes' sons before the lines in revenge. After a hard fight the Egyptians broke and fled to Memphis.

Herodotus 3.10-11

The herald's ship and the siege

Ancient account

Cambyses sent a ship of Mytilene up the Nile with a herald to offer terms. The Egyptians poured out of the walls, wrecked the ship and tore its crew apart. Memphis was besieged and in time surrendered. The Libyans and the Greeks of Cyrene and Barca sent gifts at once.

Herodotus 3.13

The test of Psamtik

Ancient account

Herodotus tells how Cambyses sat Psamtik by the road while his daughter passed dressed as a slave and his son was led to execution. Psamtik did not weep until he saw an old friend begging. Spared, he later plotted revolt and was put to death. Herodotus adds that the Persians usually left a defeated king's son to rule.

Herodotus 3.14-15

Pharaoh Mesutira

In the record

Udjahorresnet, an admiral of the last pharaohs, tells on his statue that Cambyses made him chief physician and that he composed the king's throne name, Mesutira, 'offspring of Ra'. At his request Cambyses cleared the foreigners camped in the temple of Neith at Sais, restored its incomes and worshipped there like any pharaoh.

The Apis bull

Disputed

Herodotus says Cambyses, mad, stabbed the sacred Apis bull in the thigh and it died. But a stela from the bulls' galleries at Saqqara records an Apis buried with honours in Cambyses' sixth year, 524, in a granite coffin the king gave, with Cambyses kneeling before the bull in Egyptian dress. The next Apis lived until Darius's fourth year. Most historians think no bull fits Herodotus's story.

In the game: The Apis vault and the granite barges from Elephantine are the level's great work.

Herodotus 3.27-29; Apis stelae from the Serapeum (Louvre)

Why the priests remembered him badly

In the record

A decree of Cambyses, copied centuries later on the back of the Demotic Chronicle, cut the royal payments to most of Egypt's temples. In 407 the Jews of Elephantine wrote that when Cambyses came he destroyed the temples of Egypt's gods but spared theirs. Angry priests were Herodotus's informants.

Demotic Chronicle, verso (Paris); Elephantine papyri, petition of 407 BCE

Beyond Egypt

Ancient account

Cambyses planned to sail on Carthage, but the Phoenicians would not fight their own colony, and without them the fleet was too weak. He marched south against the Ethiopians, and the march turned back when the food ran out.

Herodotus 3.17-25

The army lost in the sand

Later legend

Herodotus heard from the oasis people that fifty thousand men sent against the oracle of Ammon vanished in a sandstorm. No trace has ever been proven, though explorers have claimed finds.

Herodotus 3.26

The cats of Pelusium

Later legend

Polyaenus, writing about 700 years later, says Cambyses set cats, dogs, sheep and ibises before his army, and the Egyptians would not shoot for fear of hurting sacred animals. Herodotus, who visited Egypt and the battlefield, says nothing of it.

Polyaenus, Stratagems 7.9 (2nd century CE)

Skulls on the field

Ancient account

Herodotus walked the field of Pelusium decades later and saw the bones still lying in two heaps. The Persian skulls, he says, broke with a pebble, and the Egyptian ones would hardly crack with a stone, because Egyptians shaved their heads in the sun and Persians wore felt caps. The bones he saw; the reason is his own.

Herodotus 3.12

The spear-bearer

Ancient account

In Memphis, Syloson of Samos gave his red cloak to a young spear-bearer of the guard: Darius, son of Hystaspes. Years later, as King, Darius repaid him with Samos. Cambyses died in Syria in 522 on his way home, of a wound in the thigh; Darius's own inscription says only that he died, in words that may mean 'his own death' or 'by his own hand'.
