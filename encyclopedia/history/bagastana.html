The Lie

The cliff at Bagastana, Behistun, where Darius cut his account above the road.

Encyclopedia › The History

In 522 the empire nearly broke apart. Cambyses died, a king calling himself Bardiya held the throne, and after Darius and six nobles killed him, nine rebel kings rose across the empire. Darius told his story himself, in three languages, on a cliff at Bagastana, 'the place of the gods'.

The narrator

At Bagastana, the place of the gods, Darius had his victories over the rebel kings cut into the cliff in three languages.

The king who may have been a lie

Disputed

Darius says Cambyses had secretly killed his brother Bardiya, and that a magus named Gaumata took his name and his throne. Herodotus tells the same tale. Many historians suspect the king really was Bardiya, and that Darius, a distant cousin, seized the throne and needed a story. No other source can settle it.

Behistun inscription sections 10-13; Herodotus 3.61-79

The seven

In the record

Darius names the six men who stood with him: Intaphernes, Otanes, Gobryas, Hydarnes, Megabyzus and Ardumanish. On 29 September 522 they killed the king in the fortress of Sikayauvati in Media. Their families stayed at the top of the empire for generations.

Behistun inscription sections 13, 68

Nineteen battles, nine kings

In the record

Elam, Babylon, Media, Persia, Armenia, Sagartia and Margiana all rose. Darius and his generals fought, he says, nineteen battles in one year and took nine kings, each named and dated on the rock.

Fravartish of Media

In the record

Fravartish claimed descent from the old Median kings. Darius beat him at Kunduru and caught him at Raga. He was mutilated, chained at the palace gate for all to see, and impaled at Ecbatana. The inscription meant the lesson to be read.

Cutting the cliff

In the record

High on the rock Darius stands with his foot on the fallen magus, nine bound kings before him and the winged figure of Auramazda above. The text is in Elamite, Babylonian and Old Persian. The ledge below was cut away when the work was done, so no one could climb up to deface it.

Behistun inscription; Briant (2002) chapter 3

Copies for every land

In the record

Darius says he sent copies of the text to every land. Pieces of a Babylonian copy on stone were found at Babylon, and an Aramaic copy on papyrus, made about a century later, at Elephantine in Egypt.

Behistun inscription section 70; Elephantine Aramaic papyrus of the inscription

Rawlinson on the ledge

In the record

Between 1835 and 1847 the British officer Henry Rawlinson copied the inscription from ladders and ropes; a Kurdish boy swung across the rock to take paper casts of the Babylonian text. The Old Persian helped scholars read Babylonian cuneiform, and with it the libraries of ancient Mesopotamia.

H. C. Rawlinson, Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society 10 (1847)

The neighing horse

Later legend

Herodotus says the seven agreed that whoever's horse neighed first at sunrise would be king, and that Darius's groom made sure it was his. It is a good story; Darius's own account gives his descent from Achaemenes as his right.

Herodotus 3.84-87; Behistun inscription sections 1-4
